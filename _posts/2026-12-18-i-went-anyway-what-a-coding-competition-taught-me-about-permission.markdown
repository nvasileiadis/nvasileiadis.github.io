Picture a dark room in Leeds. Loud music, a crowd with drinks, and a row of developers on stage staring at code editors with the preview switched off. That's Code in the Dark: you get a screenshot of a finished web page and fifteen minutes to recreate it in raw HTML and CSS — no preview, no DevTools, no measuring, no undo-and-peek. When the timer runs out, everyone's attempt is projected to the room, mostly to laughter, occasionally to something genuinely impressive, and the crowd cheers for a winner. It is the least serious format in web development, and one of the best evenings the industry has ever invented.

In spring 2017 I was standing in that room as a contestant. And scrolling the contestant list before the event, I noticed something that made me smile: among the names were developers from the agency I'd worked at the year before — the same agency where, twelve months earlier, I'd pitched exactly this event and been laughed out of the room.

## The pitch

In 2016 I was a front-end developer at a busy agency. Code in the Dark was coming to Leeds for the first time, and I thought we should be there. It was free. It was an evening. It was precisely our craft, performed live, in front of the city's whole web community. I made the case to leadership.

They laughed. Not cruelly — I want to be fair to the room. If you're running an agency on billable hours and a developer proposes an event where people write code they cannot see, for no client, judged by crowd noise, I understand how it sounded. The answer was no, and the no came wrapped in the particular kind of amusement that tells you the meeting was over before you finished your sentence.

Here's the part I'm glad about, nine years later: I went anyway.

I entered as a contestant, on my own time. And because the event lists every contestant with their photo, job title, and employer, the entry page said the agency's name under mine. I put it there myself. They hadn't backed the idea, but that's whose developer I was, and representing them accurately felt like the right thing to do. It cost them nothing and it cost me nothing — an evening, a bit of nerve, fifteen minutes of squinting at un-previewed CSS in front of strangers.

## The following spring

A year later the event ran again. By then I'd moved on — a product company, a job I'd fought hard to get — and I entered again, new employer's name under my photo this time. And there, on the same contestant page, was my old agency. They'd sent developers. Officially, this time. Somebody inside that building had made the same pitch I'd made, a year later, and heard yes.

I want to be precise about what I felt, because this is where stories like this usually curdle. It wasn't vindication, or not mostly. I was genuinely pleased to see them — they were good people, and it was a good event, and the company had arrived at the right answer. It had just taken twelve months. That's the honest lesson, and it's smaller and more useful than a grudge: **the distance between "no" and "obviously" is usually about a year.** The organisation catches up. It almost always catches up. The only question is what you do with the year in between.

## The pattern, because it isn't just my story

I've told this story to enough engineers to know how common the shape is. Someone asks to attend a meetup, speak at a conference, run a lunch-and-learn, represent the company at a community event. The request is small — an evening, a ticket, sometimes just permission to say the company's name out loud. And it gets read as a distraction: from the sprint, from the client, from the real work.

I understand the reading. I've since sat on the other side of the desk, with the billable hours and the deadlines, and I know the reflex. But I've also seen what the no actually costs, and it's never the evening.

The people who ask are, almost by definition, your most self-directed. Nobody assigns community involvement; it's volunteered. When the answer is amusement, what those people learn is that their initiative is a nuisance — and self-directed people are exactly the ones with options. The no doesn't stop them going. It just decides whose name is under their photo when they do.

And the flip side, for the engineer holding the request: the trap is treating the no as final. I could have filed the idea away as rejected and stayed home. Instead, three screenshots exist — 2016, one employer's name under my face; 2017, a different employer's name; the year after that, no name at all, just me in the crowd, because by then attending was simply what I did. The [competitions line on my credentials page](/credentials/) records the same three years in one row. Lay those artifacts side by side and the lesson reads itself: **the logo under the photo changes. The person doesn't.**

Everything I invested in the community stayed with me through every logo change — the people I met at that event are colleagues, referees, and friends a decade later. Employers don't own your community involvement. They borrow it, one logo at a time, and the borrowing is a gift you're giving them whether they noticed or not.

## What I do about it now

These days I'm the one who gets pitched. Engineers ask about a conference, a meetup, an evening event that produces no ticket movement and no client deliverable. My rule is simple: this is the cheapest yes in engineering leadership, and I spend it freely.

Cheapest, because look at the price list. A conference ticket and an afternoon costs less than one recruiter fee, and it's the recruiter fee you're gambling with. A team that shows up at community events recruits for you, keeps your engineers' craft sharp on someone else's stage, and tells the people inside the building that having energy beyond the backlog is a feature, not a flight risk. The only thing I ask in return is that they bring something back — a talk summary, a tool worth trying, an introduction. They always do. Usually more than that.

I kept going, for what it's worth. The community habit that got laughed at in 2016 became conference talks, then co-organising a developer festival in this same city, then the community being — quietly, repeatedly — where the best parts of my career came from. None of that was on the roadmap the year someone found the idea funny. It rarely is.

If you're the engineer with the small request and the amused no: go anyway. Put the logo on the entry honestly, do the thing on your own time, and let the organisation take its year to catch up. It isn't defiance; it's just refusing to let someone else's timeline become yours.

And if you're the leader holding the request: it's an evening. Say yes before the meeting ends. The alternative is finding out at next year's event whose name is under their photo.
