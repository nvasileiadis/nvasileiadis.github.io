Restructurings are when people leave. Every engineering leader knows the pattern: the announcement lands, the org chart redraws itself, and within six months your best engineers — the ones with options — have quietly updated their CVs and gone. Post-reorg attrition is so normal that most companies budget for it.

Over the last three years, my team went through three company restructurings. Nobody resigned. Not during the announcements, not during the uncertainty in between, not after the dust settled. In my entire time as an engineering manager, no one has ever chosen to leave my team.

Before I explain what I actually did, let me pre-empt the fair challenge — because if I were reading this, I'd raise it too.

## "100% retention? But your team got smaller."

Correct. Across those three restructurings, my team went from eight engineers at its peak to a specialist core of two. Every one of those reductions was an organisational decision — boundaries redrawn, engineers moved to where the company needed them. Not one was a resignation.

That distinction matters more than it might seem. Organisational reshaping is leadership's prerogative; I can influence it, argue against it (sometimes successfully — more on that later), but I don't control it. What I do control is whether people *choose* to stay. Voluntary attrition is the honest measure of whether an engineer trusts their manager, sees a future, and feels their work matters. On that measure: zero, through three consecutive rounds of exactly the kind of upheaval that normally empties a team.

## Three reorgs in three years — the short version

To make the practices concrete, you need the shape of what we went through.

**The first restructuring orphaned us.** The team was moved out of its home department and renamed, and for a while nobody could say where we belonged — leadership was genuinely still working out where a content platform team should sit. What saved that period from being demoralising was that we had a mission bigger than our org position: we were benchmarking CMS vendors for what became the company's enterprise CMS. The organisation even assigned us a temporary head — a very senior programme manager — specifically so we'd have the negotiating weight to evaluate vendors and sign an enterprise contract. The contract was signed; the temporary head moved on, mission accomplished. A team without a home can still have a purpose, and purpose turned out to matter more.

**The second restructuring moved us into a platforms department**, sitting alongside teams whose work had almost nothing to do with ours. It was also the round where I was promoted into engineering management, and where the team grew — including two mid-level engineers who hadn't found the right home elsewhere in the organisation. More on how that worked below, because it's the practice I'd defend hardest.

**The third was the brutal one.** We were moved under a customer-facing pillar and our scope roughly quadrupled overnight — services and systems that had been abandoned before my tenure, some of which hadn't worked in years, suddenly became ours, while the work we were actually specialists in was pushed down the priority list (at one point the plan was for us to retrain as mobile engineers). My entire brief for this new scope was, and I am not exaggerating, a screenshot of a Miro board. On top of that, I was given the Design Systems team as a second team to run. It's one thing to be handed a title; it's another to discover the title quietly contains five more services. That year was the hardest of my career.

When the dust finally settled, we landed where we are now: a specialist core — two senior engineers and me — running six platform services for the whole organisation. There's no product manager, partly because I repositioned us as a *soft platform*: infrastructure that other teams build on, planned and prioritised with the team directly. We survived. Everyone still has their job, and everyone chose to keep it.

Here's what I actually did across those three years.

## 1. Recruit with the truth, and the truth retains

My team never had a job posting. The two engineers who joined during the second restructuring had been hired for a different part of the organisation, and the teams there turned them down. A Head of Engineering asked whether I wanted them. Instead of reading CVs, I asked for a conversation — I wanted to know who they were as people. They were decent people. So I made the honest pitch: here's exactly what the work is — building components, running content platforms, the unfashionable engineering that keeps a company's digital estate alive — are you genuinely up for that? Both said yes. Both stayed through everything that followed.

Here's the part that vindicates hiring the person rather than the label: both of their paper levels turned out to be wrong, in opposite directions. One, hired as mid-level, was really operating at junior level — so the team mentored them up, deliberately and visibly, and the engineers doing the mentoring built their own leadership credentials in the process. The other, also mid-level on paper, was senior in everything but title — two decades of experience in an adjacent domain — and became our tracking expert almost overnight, closing a capability gap I hadn't yet worked out how to fill.

Retention doesn't start when someone hands in notice; it starts in the first conversation. People leave gaps between expectation and reality. If you never create the gap, you never have to manage the disappointment — and people other teams overlooked remember who bet on them.

## 2. Say what you know. Say what you don't. Say when you'll know more.

The thing that destroys teams during a restructuring isn't the restructuring — it's the information vacuum. In the absence of facts, engineers write their own story, and the story is always worse than reality.

My rule in every round was the same three-part sentence: *here's what I know, here's what I don't know, and here's when I expect to know more.* Delivered in the team meeting, repeated in one-to-ones, updated the moment anything changed. When I genuinely knew nothing, I said "I know nothing new" — on schedule, every time. A predictable drumbeat of honest updates, including empty ones, is what tells a team their manager isn't hiding anything.

## 3. Translate the business — engineers can handle it

Restructurings come wrapped in decisions that look arbitrary from inside a team: headcount freezes, budget shifts, an AI experiment mandated from above, a scope increase nobody asked for. The default management move is to pass these down as edicts. I treated them as things to *explain*.

Because I've invested in the commercial side of engineering — budgets, CapEx/OpEx, vendor economics — I could sit with the team and walk through *why* the company was making each call: what a hiring decision costs, what the AI experiment was actually testing, why our scope grew when a department dissolved. I didn't always agree with the decisions, and I didn't pretend to. But engineers who understand the business logic of a change can metabolise it. Engineers who are handed unexplained edicts start reading job boards. Treating people like adults who can handle the real reasons is the cheapest retention tool that exists.

## 4. Careers don't pause because the org chart is moving

The quietest way to lose someone during a reorg is for them to conclude their growth is on hold until things "settle down." Things never settle down.

So I kept the machinery running through all three rounds: development conversations on schedule, and promotion cases — Associate to Mid-level, Mid-level to Senior — built on evidence rather than adjectives: delivery metrics, documented ownership, concrete outcomes, argued across organisational boundaries to decision-makers who'd never worked with the person. A promotion case fought *during* a restructuring is worth three fought in peacetime, because it proves the commitment is real when it's inconvenient.

## 5. Turn ambiguity into a map

When your brief is a screenshot of a Miro board, you have two options: panic, or treat it as an archeology project. We chose archeology. I dug through old documentation, interviewed everyone who had ever touched the abandoned systems, asked the same questions repeatedly until the answers converged, and mapped the true scope of what we'd inherited — because there's a profound difference between being told a project's title and knowing that the title conceals five more services.

Where the map revealed things we didn't know how to run, we upskilled deliberately — structured learning, then teaching each other, then documenting it for whoever came next. The team never faced the ambiguity raw; they faced a map with some regions marked "here be dragons, training scheduled Tuesday." Ambiguity is only corrosive when it's unbounded. Bounded, named, and scheduled, it becomes just work.

## 6. Anchor identity to ownership, not the org chart

Reporting lines changed three times. What didn't change: who owned what. Each engineer owned services end to end — the architecture decisions, the RFCs, the stakeholder relationships, the technical presentations to other teams. Their professional identity was anchored to their craft and their ownership, not to a box on an org chart.

You cannot promise people their org position, so anchor them to something you *can* protect. A team whose sense of worth lives in the reporting structure is destroyed by every reorg. A team whose sense of worth lives in what they've built barely notices the lines moving.

## What it cost

Honesty requires this section. Being the shock absorber has a price, and the manager pays it. The year of quadrupled scope meant absorbing product management and on-call alongside engineering management. The promotion cases fought across organisational boundaries consumed real political capital — as did one structural decision I challenged upward and got reversed: a plan to dissolve the Design Systems team and absorb it into mine. I made the case with diagrams, because a well-drawn picture wins arguments that a complaint never will. The postscript arrived nearly two years later: the preserved team's work underpinned a substantial R&D tax-credit claim, and the capability I spent political capital defending went on to enable the company-wide rebrand. Sometimes the return on holding your ground takes years to show up on anyone's books — hold it anyway. And holding a steady drumbeat of calm, honest communication while uncertain myself was a discipline, not a mood. I'd make the same trades again — but if you're an EM planning to do this, plan your own recovery too. You can't hold ground for others while running on empty indefinitely.

## The business case, for the sceptics

If the human argument doesn't move your leadership, the financial one should. Replacing an experienced engineer typically costs six to nine months of salary once you account for recruitment, ramp-up, and lost momentum — before you price in the institutional knowledge that walks out the door. Three restructurings with normal post-reorg attrition would have cost my organisation several hundred thousand pounds in replacement costs alone.

Instead, the retained core — smaller, but intact and deeply experienced — went on to deliver the company's largest recent digital programme: a full rebrand of a 522-page estate, on the critical path for every other team's launch, shipped in two quarters. That's what retention buys you. Not comfort. Capability, exactly when it's needed most.

## What I'd tell another EM

- **Recruit with the truth.** The gap between expectation and reality is where resignations are born; never create the gap.
- **Communicate on a schedule, not when you have news.** The empty update is the trust-building update.
- **Explain the business, don't relay edicts.** Engineers who understand *why* can metabolise almost any change.
- **Fight a promotion case during the chaos.** It's the loudest possible signal that careers haven't paused.
- **Map the ambiguity.** Bounded and scheduled, it's just work. Unbounded, it corrodes everything.
- **Anchor identity to ownership, not org position.** You can't protect the org chart; you can protect what people own.

People don't stay because nothing changes. They stay because someone holds the ground steady while everything changes.
